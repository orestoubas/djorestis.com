The corporate event music playbook

How to programme music around speeches, awards and a room of fifteen nationalities — from a DJ who works Brussels corporate events.

A practical guide for event organisers, HR and office managers, and agencies

By Orestis — DJ, Brussels

Why music is the most underplanned part of a corporate event

I've played corporate events in Brussels for six years — year-end parties, summer receptions, anniversaries, launches, awards evenings — for major international firms, institutions and agencies. Before that, three seasons in Mykonos nightlife; since, four years as resident DJ at Papillon Schuman.

Here's a pattern I see constantly. The venue is chosen a year ahead, the catering tasted twice, the programme rehearsed. And the music — which fills more hours of the evening than anything else, and is what people describe afterwards when they say whether it was a good party — gets decided in one line of an email three weeks out: "and we'll need a DJ from 21:00."

Music simply feels like a commodity, and it isn't. A corporate event is a harder DJ job than a wedding: an unfamiliar mixed audience, a fixed programme you don't control, hierarchy in the room, a hard finish time. What you're buying is judgement about all of that, not a speaker and a laptop.

The corporate event arc

Arrival and welcome drinks

What it needs: to make an empty room feel like it's already going. The first thirty guests arrive into a space too big for them, and music is the only thing that fills it.

What works: warm, mid-tempo, low enough to talk over without effort.

Common mistakes: starting the music when guests arrive rather than fifteen minutes before; and volume, because if people raise their voices they drift towards the exits and the room never gathers.

Dinner or seated programme

What it needs: to hold a fragmented room together across courses, table changes and speeches.

What works: a slow, deliberate lift — quieter than the reception at first, then course by course, so the room is leaning forward before anyone announces dancing.

Common mistakes: flat background for two hours. The room stays at the temperature it started, and then you ask it to jump.

Speeches and awards

What it needs: treating as a production, not an interruption.

What works: microphones tested with the actual speakers before guests arrive, walk-on music for award recipients so the room applauds rather than watches, and cue signals agreed between organiser, MC and DJ.

Common mistakes: assuming the DJ's system covers speech sound when nobody said so; handheld versus lapel never decided; speeches overrunning with no plan for the knock-on.

The transition to dancing

This is where most corporate events fail, and it deserves its own paragraph.

The moment is structurally difficult. Lights up, people seated, the CEO has just finished, half the room deciding whether to go home, and someone expects a dancefloor to appear. Handled as a hard switch — speeches end, dance music starts — you get a long, visible dead zone, and once a corporate room has decided it isn't a party, it doesn't change its mind.

What works is starting the transition before it's visible. In the last part of dinner I'm already lifting tempo and familiarity. When the programme ends I don't jump to peak-time music — I go to something recognisable and mid-tempo that gives permission rather than issuing an instruction. Meanwhile the room has to physically change: lights down, tables cleared or reconfigured, bar visible and staffed. That's a coordination job between DJ, venue and organiser, agreed in advance.

The other half is people. Every company room has fifteen or twenty guests who will dance first if the music gives them a reason — find them early. Filling a floor is the hard part; keeping it full is much easier.

The close

What it needs: a decision. Corporate events almost always have a hard finish — venue curfew, transport, the following working day.

What works: knowing the real end time, protecting the last thirty minutes as the peak rather than a fade, and ending on something deliberate. A hard stop at a high point beats twenty minutes of thinning out.

Common mistakes: nobody knowing whether the finish time is genuine or negotiable, and nobody holding authority to extend it.

Working around your programme

A DJ at a corporate event is a supporting act to your programme, and the brief should reflect that. What I need from an organiser, in order of importance:

A running order with real times. Not the aspirational one — if drinks are scheduled for 18:30 but sessions never end before 18:50, say so.

Who holds the schedule on the night. One name, one phone number. When speeches overrun — and they do — someone decides what gets compressed, and it shouldn't be the DJ guessing.

Every microphone moment, listed. Welcome, CEO, awards, external speaker — each is a technical cue.

Surprises, in advance. A flashmob, a video, a live act, a leaving tribute. I won't spoil it, but a surprise nobody briefed is a technical failure with an audience.

The tone you want. "Elegant, not a nightclub" and "we want them dancing on tables" produce entirely different evenings.

Anything to avoid. Sensitivities, recent departures, an in-joke track gone sour. Two minutes here prevents the one awkward moment people remember.

The international-audience problem

Brussels rooms are not one audience. A typical event I play has fifteen nationalities, three working languages, an age spread from graduate intake to partner level, and no shared musical childhood at all.

The default failure is to programme to the majority and lose everyone else — one country's radio hits from one decade, while a third of the room stands at the bar. What I do instead:

Lead with rhythm, not nostalgia. Nostalgia is national; rhythm isn't. Afro, Latin and house material works across a mixed room because it doesn't require anyone to have heard it before — the backbone of almost every international event I play.

Use recognition as punctuation, not structure. Big shared tracks work as peaks every fifteen or twenty minutes, not as the whole set. Between them, keep the room moving on groove.

Rotate the origins. Across an evening I'll touch Greek, Latin, Afro, RnB and European pop, deliberately spread, so no group waits an hour for something that feels like theirs.

Watch, don't assume. Which tables filled first, which languages I hear at the bar, who's dancing at the edges — it changes what I play inside the first twenty minutes.

Speak the room's languages. I work in Greek, English, French and Dutch. Announcements and award walk-ons land differently in the language a person actually thinks in.

What to check before hiring

For anyone who has to justify a supplier internally, this is the due-diligence list.

  • Public liability insurance — ask for the certificate, not the assurance. Many Brussels venues require it on file before load-in.
  • Backup equipment — a second controller or laptop, spare cables, a redundant music library. The question isn't whether equipment fails; it's how long recovery takes.
  • Venue technical coordination — will the DJ contact the venue directly about power, load-in, curfew and noise limits? Some venues have a decibel limiter, and discovering that on the night is a ruined party.
  • Named performer — will the person you're speaking to be the person who turns up?
  • Invoicing and VAT — a proper Belgian invoice with a VAT number, terms your finance team can process, and a PO reference if you need one. Prices quoted excluding VAT should say so.
  • A written scope — hours on site, setup and breakdown, equipment included, overtime rate, travel.
  • Single supplier versus multiple — if DJ, sound, lighting and photo/video come from four companies, someone on your team becomes the integrator on the night. Usually you, during the event you're hosting.

Pre-event briefing checklist

The questions I'll ask. Having the answers ready makes any DJ better, including one who isn't me.

The basics — date, venue and room, guest count, start and hard finish time, load-in access and time.

The audience — nationalities and rough proportions, age spread, whether partners or clients attend.

The programme — running order with times, microphone moments, awards and walk-ons, surprises, who holds the schedule.

The technical — venue-provided sound or lighting, decibel limiter, power at the DJ position, stage or lectern, screen and who drives it.

The intent — what success looks like at 23:00, the tone, and what must not happen.

The practical — invoicing entity, PO requirements, on-site contact and mobile, parking and access for suppliers.

Budget

What actually drives the cost of the music at a corporate event:

Hours on site. Setup usually begins several hours before guests arrive; playing time is a fraction of the day.

Room size and guest count. Sound for eighty people in a restaurant is not sound for four hundred in a warehouse. I cover up to 500 guests.

Scope beyond the dancefloor. Speech sound, award walk-ons and a technician's attention during the programme are real additions.

Production and logistics. Lighting that makes a corporate room look like an event rather than a lit conference space is the biggest visual upgrade available, and the cheapest relative to its effect. Add venues outside Brussels, late finishes and tight weekday get-out windows.

My rates start at €600 excluding VAT for DJ only; full production — sound, lighting, photo and video — is quoted to the room and guest count.

The case for one supplier. When DJ, sound, lighting and photo/video come from one place, the coordination happens between people who already work together, before the day. One contract, one invoice, one number to call when something needs solving at 19:45. For an organiser who also has to greet a CEO, host clients and manage a caterer, that's usually worth more than the line-item comparison suggests.
