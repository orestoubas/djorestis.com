Planning music for a corporate event: my playbook

A company party is three or four different events wearing one name — and the music has to change costume with each of them.

Corporate events are the most misunderstood bookings in my calendar. People imagine they are easy — "just play background music, then some hits" — when in reality they are the most agenda-driven work I do. A wedding has one emotional arc; a corporate evening has an agenda with speeches, awards, a CEO who wants energy but not too much energy, and two hundred colleagues who did not choose to spend Friday night together. Making that room feel like a party is craft. Here is how I plan it.

Read the agenda like a score

Before any corporate booking in Brussels, I ask for the full run of show: welcome, speeches, dinner or walking dinner, awards, dancing, official end time. Then I map music phases onto it. Every transition in the agenda is a musical transition too, and the events that feel effortless are the ones where those were planned, not improvised.

The four phases of a company party

  1. Arrival and welcome drinks. Volume low, tempo relaxed, personality present. This is where I earn trust with the older half of the room: quality soul, jazz-touched pop, tasteful lounge. The test is simple — can two colleagues who barely know each other hold a conversation without leaning in? If not, I am too loud.
  2. Dinner and speeches. Music becomes furniture, in the best sense. And this is where microphone discipline matters: I manage the wireless mics, cut music cleanly for every speaker, and bring it back under the applause so the room never falls into that awkward silence. In my years playing receptions in the EU quarter — including my residency at Papillon Schuman, five minutes from the Commission — I learned that a smooth speech handover impresses organisers more than any mixing trick.
  3. The ignition hour. The hardest phase. Nobody wants to be the first colleague dancing. My approach: build with universally loved tracks across eras, watch for the three or four natural movers in every company, and give them a song they cannot refuse. Once eight people dance, eighty will.
  4. The open floor. Now it is a real party — but a corporate one, so I keep lyrics workplace-safe and energy inclusive. The goal is the marketing intern and the CFO on the same floor. It happens more often than you would think.

Brief your DJ on the company, not just the songs

The most useful corporate briefing I ever receive is cultural, not musical. Tell your corporate event DJ:

  • Languages in the room. A Brussels office party can be thirty nationalities; internationally legible hits work harder than local ones.
  • The hierarchy temperature. Is leadership dancing? If the directors leave at 22:00, the real party starts at 22:05 — I plan for that.
  • Any anthem or inside joke. Many teams have one song. Played at the right moment, it does more than an hour of hits.
  • What last year was like. Too stiff? Too chaotic? I calibrate against it.

The practical checklist for organisers

  • Share the run of show at least a week ahead, with speech order and names.
  • Decide who gives the DJ live timing updates on the night — one person, not five.
  • Confirm venue sound limits and end time in writing.
  • If you want an aftermovie for internal comms or LinkedIn, plan photo and video from the start — the best corporate footage is of colleagues genuinely dancing, and that has to be captured, not staged.

The quiet metric of success

My favourite measure of a corporate event: how many people are still there thirty minutes before the end. When the room stays full to the final song, HR has won the year. If you are organising a company party or reception in Brussels or anywhere in the Benelux, have a look at my corporate events page or tell me about your agenda via the contact form — I will sketch a musical run of show for it.
