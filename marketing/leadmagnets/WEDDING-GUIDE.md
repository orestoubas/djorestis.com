# The Wedding Music Guide

### How to plan the music for your wedding day — from a DJ who has spent nine years doing it

By Orestis — DJ, Brussels
[djorestis.com](https://djorestis.com) · info@djorestis.com

---

## Why the timeline matters more than the playlist

Almost every couple I meet starts the music conversation with a playlist — a shared document, colour-coded, forty songs long, built over months. It's a lovely thing to make, and I always read it properly.

But here is what nine years has taught me — three seasons in Mykonos nightlife, six years in Brussels, four of them as resident at Papillon Schuman, plus two turns as host DJ of the Brussels Greek Food Festival: the playlist is almost never the reason a wedding dancefloor works or doesn't. The timeline is.

A song that empties the room at 21:00 will fill it at 00:30. A brilliant track played while the last table is still eating dessert does nothing at all. The same forty songs, in a different order, at different moments, produce completely different evenings. What you are really planning is not a list of songs — it's a shape for the night.

This guide is that shape. It's what I'd tell you over coffee if you came to me eighteen months out and asked where to start. Use what's useful, ignore the rest.

## The wedding day music timeline

### 1. Ceremony

**What it needs:** Three moments, and only three: the entrance, the signing or interlude, the exit. Each is short and exposed — everyone is silent and listening, so anything slightly wrong is very obvious.

**What works:** Instrumental or acoustic versions for the entrance. Something with lift for the exit — this is the first applause of the day, and the music should be pushing it, not trailing it.

**Common mistakes:** Choosing a song with lyrics that don't survive close listening (check the second verse). Underestimating how long an aisle walk takes — a two-minute song and a ninety-second walk means an awkward fade. And assuming the church or town hall has a sound system. Very often it doesn't.

### 2. Drinks reception

**What it needs:** Presence without demand. Guests are meeting each other, often across languages. The music is furniture — good furniture, but furniture.

**What works:** Warm, mid-tempo, familiar-adjacent. This is where I place the first light Greek, Latin or Afro textures if that's part of your story — instrumental enough that nobody feels a theme is being announced, but it plants a seed for later.

**Common mistakes:** Too loud — if guests raise their voices to talk, the whole reception gets tiring. And starting the party set here: if you peak at 17:30 you climb the same hill twice.

### 3. Dinner

**What it needs:** To hold the room together across a long, fragmented stretch — courses, speeches, people moving between tables.

**What works:** A slow build. I start dinner softer than the drinks reception and lift the energy course by course, so that by dessert the room is already leaning forward. Speeches need their own handling: music down fully, microphone tested beforehand, clean re-entry afterwards.

**Common mistakes:** Flat volume for two hours. And underestimating dinner length — Belgian wedding dinners routinely run longer than planned, and a DJ without a deep dinner set starts repeating or reaching for party tracks too early.

### 4. First dance

**What it needs:** To be a moment, not a performance test.

**What works:** Know your version — "the song" often exists in five recordings of different lengths and keys, so pick the exact one. Decide whether you want a shortened edit; three and a half minutes is longer than most couples expect when everyone is watching. Decide who joins you and when: parents at the second chorus, everyone at the bridge, or nobody.

**Common mistakes:** No plan for the end. The music finishes, there's applause, then a gap where the energy drains out. The transition out of the first dance deserves as much preparation as the song itself — I go straight into something that lets the room join without being told to.

### 5. The party

**What it needs:** Reading, not delivering. This part cannot be pre-planned, and any DJ who tells you they've pre-built your party set is telling you something worrying.

**What works:** Opening with your people. The first twenty minutes should be aimed at the guests most likely to dance — usually friends in their late twenties and thirties, and, at Greek weddings, everybody. A floor that is full stays full far more easily than it fills. Then widen in waves: older guests, other nationalities, other decades. A wedding dancefloor should have three or four peaks, not one long plateau.

**Common mistakes:** Playing the biggest track of the night at 23:00. Taking every request in order of arrival. Following the plan instead of the room.

### 6. The last song

**What it needs:** Deliberate closing. Somebody has to decide the night is over, and it should be you, not the venue's curfew.

**What works:** Either a big communal singalong or something slow and personal — both work, they just end the night differently. Tell your DJ which you want; it changes what he plays in the final half hour to set it up.

**Common mistakes:** Not knowing the venue's hard curfew, or whether overrunning carries a penalty. Ask early, in writing.

## The must-play / never-play worksheet

Keep it short — a focused list of ten to fifteen songs is far more useful to a DJ than a hundred. Copy the structure below and fill it in together.

**Must-play — the non-negotiables (max 10)**
Songs the night is not complete without. For each, note *when*: dinner, early party, peak, or last song.

1. …
2. …
3. …

**Family and tradition (max 5)**
Songs that belong to a specific person or moment — a parents' dance, a grandmother's favourite, a cultural set. Note who it's for.

1. …
2. …

**The banned list**
Be honest. An ex's song, an overplayed track you're sick of, a genre you can't stand. No wrong answers, and it saves an awkward moment later.

- …

**Guest requests — your policy**
Choose one: take all · take them filtered by the DJ · take none. I'd recommend the middle option, and the banned list is protected absolutely either way.

**Prompts if you're stuck**
- What was playing the night you met, or on your best holiday together?
- What did your parents dance to?
- Which three songs would empty *your* dancefloor?
- What must be playing when the room is at its loudest?

## Ten questions to ask any wedding DJ before booking

These separate professionals from people with a laptop. Ask all ten.

1. **Are you available on my date, and do you hold the date without a deposit?** Get the answer in writing.
2. **Will you personally be the DJ?** Some agencies sell a name and send whoever is free.
3. **What happens if you fall ill on the day?** A professional has a named network of colleagues and a plan. "It's never happened" is not a plan.
4. **What backup equipment do you carry?** The honest answer includes a second controller or laptop, spare cables, and a redundant copy of the music library. Failures are rare; recovery time is what matters.
5. **Do you have public liability insurance?** In Belgium many venues require proof. Ask to see it.
6. **Have you worked at my venue, and if not, will you visit or contact them?** Every room has quirks — power, curfew, acoustics, load-in.
7. **How do you handle sound for the ceremony and speeches?** Microphones are a separate question from the dancefloor.
8. **How do you decide what to play during the party?** You want to hear about reading the room, not a fixed set.
9. **What exactly is in the price, and what isn't?** Hours, travel, setup time, lighting, ceremony speaker, overtime rate, VAT.
10. **Can you work in the languages my guests speak?** For a mixed Brussels crowd this matters more than people expect.

## Mixed and multicultural weddings

This is the work I love most, and in Brussels it's most weddings — a Greek–Belgian couple, a Dutch–Italian couple, a room where a third of the guests flew in. A few things I've learned:

**Don't split the night into blocks.** The instinct is "an hour of their music, an hour of ours", and it reliably empties the floor twice. Weave instead: one continuous energy line, moving between cultures inside it, so guests are already dancing when the music shifts under them.

**Find the bridges.** Almost every pairing has overlap — rhythm, era, tempo. Afro and Latin travel remarkably well between groups because the body understands them before the ear recognises them.

**Brief both families.** Ask each side what one moment would feel wrong to skip. You usually get two or three answers, not twenty, and they're easy to honour.

**Greek specifics, since people ask me:**
- **Kalamatianó** opens the Greek set — a circle dance, communal, easy to join. Do it early in the block, and have someone lead the circle. Non-Greek guests will join if invited; they won't if nobody shows them where to stand.
- **Zeibekiko** is the opposite: solo, personal, danced *for* someone. The floor belongs to one person — nobody else dances, and nobody turns it into a spectacle unless that's the family's own custom. A DJ who doesn't know this lets three people on at once and the moment is gone. Agree in advance who gets one.
- **Tsifteteli** later, when the room is loose — it bridges beautifully into Middle Eastern and Latin material.
- Plate-smashing, sparklers, live bouzouki: all possible, all need venue permission, all to be agreed weeks in advance rather than suggested at 01:00.

## A realistic budget

Prices below are Belgian market ranges as I see them, all **excluding VAT**, for a full wedding day.

**€250–450 — hobby / part-time.** Real enthusiasm, usually a day job, entry-level equipment, limited backup. Fine for a small informal party; for a wedding, you carry the risk yourself.

**€600–700 — established professional.** This is where I start, from **€600 excl. VAT for DJ only**. Professional equipment with redundancy, insurance, venue coordination, a planning call, and someone who will still be calm at 23:00 when the schedule has moved by forty minutes.

**€900–1500+ — premium and full production.** DJ plus sound and lighting scaled to the room, ceremony and speech sound, extra staff, larger venues. I work up to 500 guests, and can bring photo and video into the same package — one supplier, one contract, one person to call.

**What drives the price:** hours on site (setup starts long before guests arrive), guest count and room size, whether ceremony and dinner need their own sound, lighting, travel, and how much planning the wedding needs. Three languages and two ceremonies is genuinely more work than a ninety-guest dinner and dancing.

**What "cheap" usually costs you:** not bad taste — bad recovery. The missing spare cable, the DJ who discovers the venue's power situation at 16:00, the fixed set that ignores your room, the unanswered phone in the final weeks, the insurance certificate the venue asks for and nobody has. The saving is a few hundred euros. The exposure is the one evening you cannot repeat.

## Booking timeline

**12–18 months out:** Venue and date, then check DJ availability immediately — good Saturdays in May, June and September go early in Belgium.

**9–12 months out:** Book the DJ. Ask the ten questions. Confirm what's included in writing.

**6–9 months out:** Ceremony music decided, and confirm whether the ceremony venue provides any sound at all.

**3–6 months out:** First dance chosen, with the exact version. Start the must-play list — don't finish it.

**6–8 weeks out:** Planning call with your DJ — timeline, speeches, surprises, cultural moments, banned list.

**2 weeks out:** Final timeline shared with venue, caterer, photographer and DJ together, so everyone works from the same document.

**The week of:** Confirm curfew, load-in and access. Then hand it over and stop planning.

### Email 2 — send day 3

**Subject:** The thing couples tell me they'd change

Hi,

When I speak to couples after their wedding, the regret is almost never about a song.

It's about the last hour.

What happens is this: the evening is going beautifully, the floor is full, and then somebody realises the venue's curfew is in forty minutes. The end arrives as an interruption rather than a finish. No last song planned, no moment, just lights on.

It's completely avoidable, and it costs you nothing. Three things:

**One — find out your venue's hard curfew now**, in writing, along with whether overrunning is possible and what it costs. Venues vary hugely across Belgium and the answer is rarely on the website.

**Two — decide what kind of ending you want.** A big communal singalong, or something slow and personal for the two of you. Both are lovely. They just need different setups, and your DJ will shape the final half hour differently depending on which you choose.

**Three — tell your DJ the actual finish time**, not the time on the schedule. Those are frequently different by half an hour.

That's it. Ten minutes of admin, and the difference between a night that ends and a night that stops.
