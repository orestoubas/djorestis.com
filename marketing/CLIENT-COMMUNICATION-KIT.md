## Why this kit exists

Enquiries are lost to slow, vague or unprofessional replies far more often than they are lost to price.

A couple who emails four DJs on a Sunday evening is not comparing four prices. They are comparing four experiences of being replied to. The one who answers within a day, asks intelligent questions and sounds like he has done this before is already ahead — usually before anyone has mentioned a number.

Everything below is written to be copied, filled in and sent. The `[square brackets]` are the only things you change. Nothing here needs rewriting to sound like you; it is already written in your voice.

**Three rules that matter more than any template:**

1. **Answer within 48 hours. Always.** It is the promise printed on your website and repeated in every guide you send out. Even when you cannot quote yet, reply inside 48 hours to say when you will. A holding reply costs two minutes and keeps the promise.
2. **Never send a bare price.** A number on its own invites comparison on the number alone. A number inside a clear scope invites a decision.
3. **Always name the next step.** Every message in this kit ends by telling the client exactly what happens next and who does it. That single habit is what makes an enquiry feel handled.

**A note on the €600 figure.** Your published starting price is **€600 excl. VAT for a DJ set, with sound and lighting quoted separately**. Everything in this kit is consistent with that. Never quote below it in writing without deciding deliberately to change your published price — the website, the wedding guide and your proposals all have to agree, or clients notice.

# 1. First reply to an enquiry

**When:** within 48 hours, ideally within 4 working hours. Speed is the single largest advantage available to you and it is free.

**What it must do:** confirm you exist and are paying attention, establish that you know the job, ask only the questions you genuinely need to quote, and say when the quote arrives.

**What it must not do:** quote a price. You cannot price an event you have not scoped, and a number sent early gets anchored against and haggled over. The one exception is a client who explicitly asks "what's your minimum?" — see §10.

**Three or four questions maximum.** Every extra question lowers the reply rate. The four that actually change the price are: **date, venue, guest count, hours**. Everything else can wait for the planning call.

# 2. The quote email

**When:** within 48 hours of receiving the answers to your questions. If you cannot make 48 hours, send a one-line holding message — *"I have everything I need; the proposal reaches you Thursday morning."* The promise is that you respond within 48 hours, and a dated commitment honours it.

**Structure, in this order, every time:** what you understood → what is included → what is not → the price → what happens next → how long the quote holds.

That order matters. By the time the reader reaches the number, they have already read what it buys.

---

## How to present price so it does not invite haggling

Six habits. They cost nothing and they change how the number lands.

**1. Never let the number stand alone on a line.** `€850` invites a counter-offer. `€850 excl. VAT — five hours of music, full setup and soundcheck, and a planning call before the day` invites a decision. Always bind the price to what it buys, in the same sentence.

**2. Quote a single figure, not a range.** A range is read as an invitation to negotiate towards the bottom of it. If the scope is genuinely uncertain, quote the price for a defined scope and list the variables as separately priced options.

**3. Itemise the scope, not the cost of each part.** Say what is included. Do not publish a line-by-line internal breakdown — it turns the conversation into "can we drop the lighting to save €120?" rather than "is this the right evening?".

**4. Always write "excl. VAT" and always show the incl. VAT figure too.** Private clients think in the final number, companies think in the excl. VAT number. Give both and neither is surprised later. A surprise at invoice stage costs you the review as well as the goodwill.

**5. Name what makes it worth it, once, in one sentence.** Not a sales pitch — a fact. *"That price reflects nine years of doing this, redundant equipment, and someone who is still calm at 23:00 when the schedule has moved by forty minutes."* Say it once and move on.

**6. Put a date on the quote's validity and on the date-hold.** Not as pressure — as information. A quote that holds forever signals a diary that is empty forever.

**And if they still push back on price:** the answer is never a silent discount. See §10a — you reduce the scope or you hold the price, but you do not quietly do the same work for less. A discount given without a reason teaches the client that your first number was not serious.

# 4. Follow-up sequence for an unanswered quote

**Why this matters.** Most quotes that go quiet are not rejections. They are a couple who got busy, a manager waiting on a budget sign-off, an organiser who forwarded it to a colleague and forgot. The DJ who follows up politely three times books events the DJ who follows up once does not.

**The rules of following up well:**

- **Short.** Every email below is under 120 words. A long follow-up reads as anxiety.
- **Never guilt.** No "I haven't heard from you", no "just checking in again". Both put the client in the wrong for something that is not their fault.
- **Give something each time.** A useful fact, a decision that helps them, a genuine deadline. Never just a nudge.
- **Three, then stop.** After day 21 you stop and let it go. Chasing a fourth time costs you the referral as well as the booking.
- **Reply in the same email thread** so the original quote is one scroll away.

# 6. Pre-event questionnaire

**When:** send 3–4 weeks before the event, and always **before** the planning call, not instead of it. The written answers make the call short and specific rather than exploratory.

**How to send it:** as the body of an email that they can reply to inline, or as an online form. Do not send a PDF they have to print. The easier it is to answer badly, the more likely it is to be answered at all — and a half-filled questionnaire is far more useful than a perfect one you never receive.

**Chase once, after a week.** One line: *"Whenever you have fifteen minutes for the questionnaire — no rush, but it makes our call much shorter."*

# 7. Day-before message

**When:** the day before, late morning or early afternoon. Not the evening — by then they are past the point of wanting information.

**How:** WhatsApp or SMS is better than email here. It is the one message that should feel like a person, not a supplier.

**What it must do:** confirm your arrival time, be short, and be calm. The client is stressed. Your job in this message is to be one thing that is definitely not going to be a problem.

> Hi [name] — Orestis here.

> All set for tomorrow. I will arrive at **[venue]** at **[time]**, and I will be fully set up and soundchecked well before your guests arrive.

> See you tomorrow.

**Corporate variant — swap the last paragraph:**

> If the running order moves on the day, just tell me on site — I would rather adjust than have the programme wait for me. See you tomorrow at [time].

# 8. Post-event: thank you and review request

**When:** the next day. Late morning is ideal — they are awake, they are still glowing, and nothing has replaced the memory yet. A week later the feeling has cooled and the review does not get written.

**The mechanics that decide whether you get a review:**

- **Ask once, plainly, in one sentence.** Not "if you have a moment, and only if you feel like it, it would mean a lot…". That apologetic framing gives them permission to skip it.
- **Give the direct link.** A Google review link that opens straight into the write-a-review box. Anything requiring them to search for you loses most people. Set this up once — see `GOOGLE-BUSINESS-PROFILE.md`.
- **Tell them what to write about.** People stall because they do not know what to say. Two suggested angles removes the blank page.
- **Say how long it takes.** "Two minutes" is a fact that gets acted on.
- **Never chase a review more than once**, and never chase at all if the event had a problem.

# 9. Review response templates

**Respond to every review. All of them, within a few days.**

An unanswered review is a conversation you did not turn up to. And remember who the audience is: **you are never really writing to the reviewer.** You are writing to the next person reading your reviews while deciding whether to enquire. That person forms their judgement mostly from how you handle the worst review on the page.

---

## 9a. Five-star review

Keep it short, make it specific, do not gush, do not sell. A long grateful reply to a short review looks needy. Three sentences is the target.

> Thank you [name] — that means a lot.

> [One specific memory of their event, proving you were actually there and that this is not a copy-paste.] [E.g. *"That last hour was something else — I do not think anyone sat down."*]

> It was a genuine pleasure. All the best to you both.

> — Orestis

**Variant — corporate:**

> Thank you [name]. It was a well-organised evening, which makes a real difference from where I stand. Glad the transition into the dancing worked as we planned it. Looking forward to the next one.
>
> — Orestis

---

## 9b. Three-star or lukewarm review

**These are the most useful reviews you will get and the ones most people handle worst.** A three-star review usually means one specific thing went wrong inside an otherwise fine evening. Your reply has one job: show the reader that you take the specific point seriously and that you are not defensive about it.

**Do:** thank them, name the specific issue back to them in your own words, say what you have changed or would do differently, offer to talk offline.
**Do not:** explain why it was not your fault, list the things that went well, or say "we always..." — you are one person, and "we" reads as a corporate shield.

> Thank you for the honest feedback [name] — I would rather have this than a polite five stars.

> You are right about [the specific issue, named plainly and without hedging]. [Then either: *"That one is on me, and I have changed how I handle it — I now [specific change]."* Or, if it was genuinely outside your control: *"That came down to [factor], which I could have flagged with you beforehand and did not. That part was mine."*]

> I am glad [the genuine positive they mentioned] worked. If you would like to talk it through properly, I would welcome that — [email / phone].

> — Orestis

---

## 9c. Negative review

### Read this before you write anything

**First: do not reply today.** A negative review triggers an urge to correct the record, and a reply written inside the first hour has never in history improved anything. Write your reply, save it, sleep, then read it again the next morning and cut a third of it. The delay costs you nothing — reviewers rarely expect a same-day response, and future clients will never know the difference.

**Second: understand what the reply is for.** You are not going to change the reviewer's mind — it almost never happens, and trying to is exactly what makes a reply look bad. **You are writing for the next twenty people who read it.** Those people are not asking "who was right?". They are asking "if something went wrong at my event, what would this person be like?". Answer *that* question and a bad review can genuinely end up costing you nothing.

**Third: the tone rules, in order of importance.**

1. **Short.** Shorter than the review. A long defence looks like a long defence, whatever it says. Four sentences, maximum six.
2. **No rebuttal of details in public.** The instant you write "actually, the timeline was changed by the venue", you have started an argument in front of an audience, and the audience always finds arguing suppliers unattractive — even when they are right. Correct the record privately, or not at all.
3. **Concede something real.** There is nearly always one thing you would do differently, even if the overall complaint is unfair. Naming it costs you nothing and buys you all your credibility.
4. **Never mention the money.** No "the full fee was paid", no "a partial refund was offered". Publicly discussing payment always looks worse for the supplier, whatever the facts.
5. **Move it offline, once.** Offer a direct conversation, with a real contact detail. Do not offer twice.
6. **No emotional language at all.** No "disappointed", no "surprised to read this", no "unfortunately". Each one signals that the review got to you.
7. **Never imply the reviewer is lying, mistaken or has the wrong supplier.** Even when true, and even gently. It reads as an attack every time.

**If the review is defamatory, contains false factual claims, or is not from a real client**, that is a different problem with a different route: report it to the platform, and if it is doing real damage, **take Belgian legal advice before responding publicly at all.** Do not attempt to fight it in the review thread.

### Template — negative review response

> Thank you for taking the time to write this, [name].

> [Concede the specific thing, in your own words, without qualifying it.] *"You are right that [X], and I understand why that took the shine off the evening."*

> [One sentence — and only one — on what you have changed.] *"I have since changed how I handle [X], so that it does not happen to anyone else."*

> [The offline offer, once.] *"I would like to talk it through with you properly rather than in a review thread. You can reach me directly on info@djorestis.com or [phone], and I will make the time whenever suits you."*

> — Orestis

### Worked example

> Thank you for taking the time to write this, [name].
>
> You are right that the music dropped out during the speeches, and on a day like yours that is the moment it least should have. That was mine to prevent and I did not.
>
> I have changed how I handle microphone hand-overs since, and I now run a second check with the speakers before the room sits down.
>
> I would rather talk this through with you properly than in a review thread — info@djorestis.com or [phone], any time that suits you.
>
> — Orestis

Notice what is absent: no explanation of *why* it happened, no mention of what went well, no mention of money, and no suggestion that the reviewer is being unfair. It is under 100 words. A reader who sees that reply and a wall of five-star reviews above it comes away thinking better of you, not worse — which is the entire objective.

# 10. Awkward situations

Five conversations that cost bookings when handled badly, and almost never when handled directly. The common thread in all five: **answer quickly, say the actual thing, and offer a real alternative.** Vagueness is what makes these situations awkward — not the subject matter.

---

## 10a. A client asks for a discount

**The principle: never discount the price without reducing the scope.** A price that drops because someone asked was never a real price, and the client now knows it — which affects every conversation you have with them afterwards, including the one about extra hours on the night. What you can always do is offer them a *smaller thing* for less money. That protects your rate, gives them a genuine choice, and often results in them buying the original package anyway.

Also: your published floor is **€600 excl. VAT for a DJ set**. You can shape a package below your quoted figure by removing scope, but do not go under your own published starting price in writing. It undermines the website, the wedding guide and every quote you will send afterwards.

> Hi [name],

> Thanks for being straightforward about it — I would rather have this conversation than lose you to silence.

> I do not discount the rate, and I would rather tell you why than dress it up: the price reflects the hours before the event as much as the hours at it — the planning, the venue coordination, the preparation, and equipment that has a backup for everything. Take money off and something in that list quietly stops happening, which is exactly the part you would notice on the day.

> What I can do is build a smaller version that genuinely fits. Two honest options:

> **Option A — €[X] excl. VAT.** [Fewer hours — start at 21:00 rather than 19:00, dinner music from the venue's own system.]
> **Option B — €[X] excl. VAT.** [DJ only; you source sound and lighting from the venue or a rental company. I will tell you exactly what to ask them for so it works with my setup.]

> Both are real options and I am happy with either. What I will not do is quote the full evening for less and hope it works out — that ends badly for both of us.

> If the budget genuinely is not there, tell me and I will point you towards someone good at that level. No hard feelings at all.

> — Orestis

---

## 10b. A date you are already booked

**Reply within 48 hours, same as any other enquiry.** This message costs you nothing and can earn you the next three bookings. A DJ who replies fast, says no clearly and recommends someone good is a DJ people remember and pass on.

> Hi [name],

> Thank you for thinking of me — and I am sorry, [date] is already booked. I do not take two events in a day, because the second one always gets the worse version of me.

> Three things that might still help:

> **If your date has any flexibility**, [adjacent dates] are open and I would be glad to be involved.

> **If it does not**, I can recommend [one or two names] — [one line on why each is good and what they are strong at]. Tell them I sent you. I only pass on people I would be comfortable being compared to.

> **And whether or not we work together this time**, the [wedding music guide / corporate playbook] on my site is free and genuinely useful for planning around whoever you book: djorestis.com/[wedding-music-guide|corporate-event-music-playbook]/

> If you are planning anything else this year, send it over early and I will hold the date properly.

> Good luck with it.

> — Orestis

## 10c. A request outside what you offer

**Say no cleanly and early.** The expensive version of this is the yes you give reluctantly to a job you are wrong for, which ends in a mediocre event and a three-star review. Your own wedding guide already says this out loud — *"I would rather point you elsewhere than take a wedding I'm wrong for"* — so saying it is entirely consistent with how you present yourself.

> Hi [name],

> Thank you for the detail — it helps, because it means I can give you a straight answer rather than a hopeful one.

> **This is not the right job for me.** [Then the honest reason, in one sentence: *"You need a live band rather than a DJ for that format."* / *"I do not do children's entertainment, and you would want a specialist."* / *"A five-hour techno set is not what I do well — I am an open-format DJ, and you would be better served by someone who lives in that world."*]

> I could take it. But you would get a competent version of something rather than a good version, and on [event] that is not a trade worth making.

> [If you know someone: *"[Name] is genuinely good at exactly this — [contact]. Say I sent you."*]
> [If you do not: *"I do not have a name for you off-hand, but if you tell me how the search goes I will ask around my network."*]

> If anything else comes up that is more my kind of evening, please do come back — I would like to work with you at some point.

## 10d. A client who goes silent after agreeing

**Diagnose before you chase.** Silence after a verbal yes usually means one of three things: they got busy, an internal approval is stuck, or something changed and they are embarrassed to say so. All three are solved by making it very easy to tell you the truth.

**Escalate in this order, and no faster:**
1. **Day 3** — a short email, framed as practical, not as chasing.
2. **Day 7** — a phone call or WhatsApp. A voice resolves in ninety seconds what email cannot in two weeks.
3. **Day 12** — the honest email below, which names the situation and gives them an easy exit.

The important thing: **until the deposit is paid, the date is not booked, and they need to understand that without being threatened with it.** State it as a fact about your diary, not as pressure.

> **Subject:** [Date] — where do we stand?

> Hi [name],

> I have not heard back since we agreed, and I want to raise it now rather than let it drift.

> Where things stand practically: **the deposit has not come through, so [date] is not formally confirmed.** I have been keeping it clear anyway, on the basis of our conversation — but I cannot keep doing that indefinitely, and I would much rather tell you that than quietly release the date and let you find out later.

> So, whichever it is:

> - **Still on?** Send the deposit whenever suits and it is done. Details are in my last email, and I can resend the invoice.
> - **Waiting on something?** Just tell me what and by when, and I will hold the date until then. That is completely fine and happens often.
> - **Changed your mind?** Also completely fine. One line back and we close it cleanly, with no awkwardness and nothing owed.

> Any of the three is a good answer. Silence is the only one I cannot work with.

> — Orestis

## 10e. A last-minute cancellation

**Two separate things happen here, and it is important not to mix them up.** The human response and the commercial one are both legitimate, and they go in that order. Lead with the money and you look like a supplier; lead with the person and you keep the relationship — often along with the money.

**Before you send anything, check what your own written terms say** (§3, section 9) and follow them. Terms you do not enforce are terms you do not have. But enforce them warmly, and always name the discretion you are choosing to exercise, so a concession reads as a decision rather than a weakness.

**If the reason is genuinely serious** — a bereavement, a serious illness, a family emergency — send only the first two paragraphs, and leave the commercial part for a separate email a few days later. Nobody has ever regretted waiting a week to talk about a deposit.

> **Subject:** [Date]

> Hi [name],

> I am sorry — genuinely. [One human sentence, matched to the reason. *"That is a horrible thing to have to deal with, and the event is the least of it."*]

> Take whatever time you need with the rest of this. But so it is written down somewhere and you do not have to think about it:

> **The practical position.** Under the terms we agreed, a cancellation [N] days out means [the exact term from your proposal — the deposit is retained / X% of the total is due]. That is because the date has been out of my diary since [month], and at this notice it will not be filled.

> **What I would rather do.** If you think there is a chance you will reschedule, **I will hold your deposit as a credit against a new date within the next [12] months**, rather than treat it as a cancellation. No deadline pressure on choosing the date — just tell me when you know.

> [If a genuine concession is being made, name it: *"And given the circumstances, I am waiving the [X]% balance that would otherwise be due. I would rather do that than be one more thing on your list this month."*]

> If you would rather just close it off cleanly instead, say so and I will confirm it in writing today and you will not hear from me about it again.

> Whichever you choose, I am sorry it turned out this way.

> — Orestis

**A note for yourself, not for the client:** a cancelled date is worth one attempt at re-filling. Post it to your Instagram stories — *"[Date] has just come free — Brussels or nearby, get in touch"* — and email any enquiry from the last three months who wanted that date or one near it. It works more often than you would expect.

# Appendix — have these professionally reviewed

Nothing in this kit is legal, tax or insurance advice, and I am not able to give any. The templates are commercial writing. Before the following go in front of a paying client, get them checked by someone qualified in Belgium.

**A Belgian accountant should review:**
- The VAT treatment on your proposals and invoices, including the reverse-charge line for VAT-registered business clients in other EU member states (§3, section 7)
- Your deposit and balance invoicing — when a VAT invoice must be issued for a deposit, and how it is credited against the final invoice
- Late-payment terms and any interest or fixed compensation you state (§3, section 8) — Belgian law on commercial transactions sets specific rules here, and the rules differ for consumers
- Whether the €600 excl. VAT starting price is presented correctly on quotes going to private individuals, who are used to seeing prices inclusive of VAT

**A Belgian lawyer, or your professional federation, should review:**
- The **cancellation table** in §3, section 9 — the percentages there are common market practice, not law, and clauses that are considered unfair against a consumer can be struck out entirely
- The **force majeure clause**, which is deliberately left unwritten in the template. Do not draft this one yourself.
- The **replacement-DJ clause** and any statement about what you owe if you cannot perform
- Any **limitation of liability** — Belgian law restricts what a supplier may exclude, particularly against consumers
- Whether your terms need to distinguish between **consumer and business clients**, which in most cases they do
- The **photography and promotional-use clause**, alongside your GDPR position for guest images

**Your insurer should confirm, in writing, before you state anything:**
- Whether you currently hold professional and public liability cover, the insured amount, and the policy number
- Whether you can produce a certificate for venues that request one
- Whether your equipment is covered in transit and on site, and what happens if a guest damages it

**Until each of those is confirmed, leave the relevant placeholder in the template empty.** An empty section costs you nothing. A confident claim you cannot back up costs you the client, the venue relationship and potentially a great deal more.

**Two things to verify yourself, once:**
- **SABAM / Unisono.** Confirm the current position on who pays music-licensing fees for private and corporate events in Belgium, and reflect it accurately in §3, section 11. Most venues handle it, but "most" is not a term to put in a contract.
- **Your published promises.** The 48-hour response time and the €600 excl. VAT starting price appear on djorestis.com, in the wedding guide, in the corporate playbook and throughout this kit. If you ever change either one, change it everywhere on the same day. A client who finds two different numbers stops trusting both.
