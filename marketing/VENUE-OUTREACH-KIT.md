## 0. Why this is the highest-return channel you have

A couple books one DJ, once, and never comes back. A venue books 40–120 weddings and corporate
events a year, forever, and its coordinator is asked *"can you recommend a DJ?"* at nearly every
site visit. One venue that puts you on its supplier list is worth more than a year of ads.

Three things make you unusually placeable on those lists, and every email below is built around them:

1. **Four languages** (Greek, English, French, Dutch). Brussels venues constantly deal with mixed
   Belgian/EU/international guest lists. A DJ who can read the room *and* address it in the right
   language is genuinely rare and is the single most persuasive line you have.
2. **Full production up to 500 guests** — DJ, sound, light, photo, video. Venues hate coordinating
   five suppliers. One invoice, one contact, one setup crew is a real operational benefit *to them*,
   not just to the client.
3. **Nine years, verifiable** — 3 seasons Mykonos, 6 years Brussels, a 4-year residency at Papillon
   Schuman, twice host DJ of the Brussels Greek Food Festival, corporate work for major
   international firms. A residency is the proof point venues care about: it means you show up,
   on time, sober, every week, for years.

**Prerequisite, and this is not optional:** before you send email number one, you need photos and a
short video of you working a full room. See `GROWTH-ROADMAP.md`. A venue coordinator will click your
site within 30 seconds of reading your email; an empty gallery kills the introduction. If you have
no media yet, spend the first two weeks getting it, then start this campaign.

## 4. Offer strategy — what to actually put on the table

The mistake almost every supplier makes is offering a discount. A discount is the one thing a venue
cannot use: it doesn't make them money, it doesn't make their life easier, and it quietly tells them
your normal price is fiction. Venues have exactly three interests — **not being embarrassed in front
of a client, filling their calendar, and looking good online.** Every offer below is scored against
those.

### Option A — Reciprocal promotion (free content trade)

You give the venue photos, video and an aftermovie from every event you play there, licensed for
their free use. They get marketing assets they'd otherwise pay €500–1 500 for. You get named on
their site and socials, plus a backlink.

- **Pros:** Costs you nothing beyond what you're already producing. Solves a real, expensive problem.
  Directly serves interest #3. Repeatable — it gets better every time you play there. Produces the
  backlinks and social proof that `BACKLINKS-ACTION-PLAN.md` and `GROWTH-ROADMAP.md` both need.
- **Cons:** Requires you to actually have a photographer/videographer, which means the production day
  must happen first. Slow — the payoff arrives after event two or three. Some venues have in-house
  marketing and won't care.
- **Verdict:** ✅ **Lead with this.** It is the only offer that gives before it asks, costs almost
  nothing, and compounds.

### Option B — A named venue rate (not a discount)

Not "10% off" — a *fixed, published, guaranteed* price for anyone the venue sends you, with an extra
deliverable rather than a lower number: ceremony and aperitif coverage included at no charge, or a
free planning meeting at the venue with the couple.

- **Pros:** Coordinators love a number they can quote from memory without checking. Protects your
  €600 floor. Gives the venue a reason to name you specifically instead of "a DJ".
- **Cons:** You must honour it, including in August when you're fully booked. Creates a second price
  list to track. If the venue leaks it, it becomes your public price.
- **Verdict:** ✅ **Pair with A.** Together they form the whole offer: "free content, and a fixed
  price your couples can trust."

### Option C — Reduced-rate first event

One event at a materially reduced rate to prove you're safe.

- **Pros:** Removes their risk entirely, which is their #1 interest. Fastest route from stranger to
  known quantity. Genuinely useful when a venue is polite but non-committal.
- **Cons:** Anchors you low, sometimes permanently. Attracts venues that will keep asking. The
  discount is invisible to the couple, who pays the venue's recommendation, not your invoice — so
  you buy goodwill from someone who isn't paying. Undercuts other DJs, which the industry notices.
- **Verdict:** ⚠️ **Hold in reserve.** Use once, maybe twice a year, only on a Tier A venue that has
  already said "we'd need to see you work first". Never advertise it in a first email — frame it as
  a solution to *their* stated hesitation, and say explicitly that it applies to the first event only.

### Option D — Free showcase evening at the venue

You play a venue open day, tasting evening, or supplier showcase for free.

- **Pros:** You're seen working *in their room* by their staff and their prospective clients at once.
  Venues run these events anyway and often struggle to fill the entertainment slot. Cheap for you —
  one evening, no discount precedent, since there was no fee to discount.
- **Cons:** Open days are daytime, low-energy, and show none of your actual skill. Very easy to be
  treated as free background music forever. Zero direct revenue.
- **Verdict:** ✅ **Say yes when offered, once per venue.** Don't build a strategy on it. Set the
  boundary in advance: "happy to do the open day free of charge — for paid events my normal rates
  apply."

### Option E — A free technical sheet for the room

You visit, measure, and write a one-page sound-and-power sheet for the venue: where the sockets are,
what the room does to bass, where a booth should go, decibel limits, load-in route.

- **Pros:** Nearly free, takes an hour, and is *unmistakably* expert. Venues keep these and hand them
  to every future supplier — with your name at the bottom. Wildly under-used.
- **Cons:** Only works if you actually know what you're doing (you do). Some venues already have one.
- **Verdict:** ✅ **The best door-opener in this list for venues that ignore your first two emails.**
  It converts "please recommend me" into "here, I made you something."

### Option F — Commission to the venue

Paying the venue a cut of your fee for referrals.

- **Pros:** Some venues and agencies expect it, and it aligns incentives crudely but effectively.
- **Cons:** It comes straight out of a €600 fee. It must be invoiced and declared properly under your
  VAT number — an undocumented kickback is a real problem, not a grey area. It changes the
  relationship from "trusted colleague" to "vendor who pays", and paying venues drop you the moment
  someone pays more.
- **Verdict:** ❌ **Don't offer it.** If a venue or agency *asks*, treat it as normal commercial
  practice, negotiate it into your price rather than out of your margin, and insist on a proper
  invoice. Never propose it yourself.

### The recommended opening position

> **A + B, always. E when they go quiet. C only when they name the objection. D when invited. F never
> unless asked.**

And one thing that outranks all of them: **be the easiest supplier they deal with.** Reply within four
hours. Send a signed contract without being chased. Arrive when you said. Leave the room cleaner than
you found it. Coordinators recommend the person who never creates work for them, long before they
recommend the best DJ.

## 5. Tracking system & weekly cadence

### 5.1 The tracker

Copy this into Google Sheets (free) — do not buy a CRM. One row per organisation, one tab for venues,
one for planners. The three columns that actually change your behaviour are **Follow-up due**,
**Stage** and **Next action**; everything else is memory.

```markdown
| Venue / Planner | Type | Tier | Contact name | Role | Email | Phone | Language | Date contacted | Channel | Response | Stage | Follow-up due | Next action | Outcome | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Salon 58 | Venue | A | | Events manager | info@salon58.com | | FR | 2026-08-11 | Email | — | 1. Contacted | 2026-08-21 | Send follow-up 3.6 | | Libre de traiteur; large community weddings |
| Château de Petit-Leez | Venue | B | | | | | FR | | Email | | 0. To contact | | Find contact name | | Site lists partner DJs — ask directly |
| Alina Vandaele | Planner | A | Alina | Founder | | | EN | | Email | | 0. To contact | | Comment on 2 posts first | | International/intercultural specialist |
```

**Stage** — use exactly these six values, nothing else:
`0. To contact` → `1. Contacted` → `2. Replied` → `3. Meeting/visit booked` → `4. On supplier list`
→ `5. Booked / played`. Add `X. Closed – not interested` and genuinely stop writing to those.

**Outcome** — fill in only at stage 4 or 5, with the date and the person's name. That is the record
you'll mine in a year's time.

Conditional formatting: highlight the row red when `Follow-up due` is in the past. That single rule
does more than any software you could buy.

### 5.2 Realistic weekly cadence (3–5 hours)

| Day | Time | Task |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | 30 min | **Research.** Take the next 5 targets. Find a real human name and address — venue website "team"/"contact" page, LinkedIn, or phone the venue and ask "who handles supplier enquiries?" A named email is worth five to `info@`. |
| Tuesday | 45 min | **Send 5 first-contact emails.** Personalise one sentence each — the venue's actual rooms, an event you know they hosted. Log all 5 in the tracker with `Follow-up due` = +10 days. |
| Wednesday | 30 min | **Clear the follow-up queue.** Send template 3.6/3.7 to everything red. Two follow-ups maximum, ever, then close the row. |
| Thursday | 45 min | **One value action.** A thank-you email with photos to a venue you played, a technical sheet for a room you've worked, a genuine comment on a planner's post, or a 20-minute venue visit. This is the part everyone skips and it is where the results come from. |
| Friday | 15 min | **Update the tracker and stop.** |

**Total: ~2h45 of committed time**, leaving 1–2 hours of slack for a coffee meeting or a site visit,
which is what the other four days are actually for.

**Volume maths, honestly.** 5 emails/week × 8 weeks covers this entire list of 39 targets. Expect
roughly **8–12 replies, 3–5 meetings, and 1–3 supplier-list placements** from the first pass — that
is a normal, good outcome, not a disappointing one. After week 8 the job changes completely: it stops
being outreach and becomes *nurture* — one thank-you email per event played, one seasonal check-in per
listed venue (late September, before the year-end corporate season; and mid-January, when couples book
summer). Put those two dates in the calendar now.

## 6. What NOT to do

The fastest ways to get ignored, or quietly marked as someone not to recommend:

1. **Don't send a mass BCC or a mail-merge with a visible `[VENUE]` left in.** It happens weekly and
   it ends the relationship on the spot. Five personalised emails beat fifty generic ones, and the
   personalisation only has to be one sentence.
2. **Don't attach anything to a first email.** A 6 MB press kit lands in spam or annoys. Link it.
3. **Don't open with what you need.** "I'm looking for work" and "I'd love the opportunity" make you
   a cost. Open with what the venue gets: four languages, one supplier instead of five, free content.
4. **Don't email `info@` when a name is findable.** Two minutes on their contact page or LinkedIn
   multiplies your reply rate.
5. **Don't undercut on price in a first email.** It is the loudest possible signal that you are not
   the person they'd risk a client's wedding on. Your €600 floor is an asset — hold it.
6. **Don't chase more than twice.** One email, one follow-up at ten days, then stop. A third and
   fourth chase moves you from "not now" to "never" and costs you the venue permanently.
7. **Don't badmouth the venue's current DJ**, ever, even mildly, even when invited to. Coordinators
   hear it as "this is what he'll say about us." Position yourself as the *additional* option for
   dates their regular can't cover or languages they can't handle.
8. **Don't turn up unannounced during an event**, or hand cards to guests at someone else's wedding.
   This is the single fastest way onto an actual blacklist, and venues warn each other.
9. **Don't go around the venue to the couple**, or around the planner to their client. Suppliers who
   disintermediate get removed from every list in the network, and Brussels is small.
10. **Don't overstate the rig.** If you say 500 guests, you must be able to cover 500 guests. One
    under-powered room in front of a coordinator ends the relationship and the reference.
11. **Don't ignore the venue's rules** — decibel limits, curfew, load-in times, no confetti, no smoke
    machine near the detectors. Breaking a noise limit costs the venue money or its permit and no
    amount of goodwill survives it.
12. **Don't be slow.** A coordinator forwarding an enquiry expects an answer the same day. If you
    reply in three days you have already lost that booking and probably the next one.
13. **Don't ask to be "kept in mind."** It is not an ask, so nobody acts on it. Ask one concrete
    question: *"Do you keep a list of recommended suppliers, and what do you need from me to be on
    it?"*
14. **Don't skip the thank-you email after you play.** It costs ten minutes and it is the highest-
    converting message in this entire document.
