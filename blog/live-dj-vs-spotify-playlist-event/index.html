Live DJ vs Spotify playlist: what each one actually does

I make my living behind the decks, so read this critically, but I promise to tell you when a playlist genuinely is enough.

Someone at every dinner party eventually says it: "Why pay for a DJ? We could just make a great playlist." It is a fair challenge, and I would rather answer it than get defensive, so here is my comparison, including the events where I think a playlist is the right call.

Where a playlist genuinely wins

Let me start against my own interest. A playlist is the better choice when:

  • Music is background, full stop. A dinner for twelve, a vernissage, a garden barbecue. If nobody is meant to dance, a well-made playlist through decent speakers is perfect.
  • The budget is truly tiny. If hiring a professional means cutting food or drink, cut the DJ. A generous party with a playlist beats a stingy one with a professional.
  • The group is small and homogeneous. Fifteen close friends with identical taste do not need a crowd-reader. They are one crowd, and they already know their songs.

If that is your event: make the playlist twice as long as the party, turn off crossfade gaps, assign one friend as guardian of the phone, and enjoy.

Where the comparison breaks down

The playlist argument rests on a hidden assumption: that the right songs, in advance, are knowable. For a real party, they are not, and this is the entire craft. Four things a playlist structurally cannot do:

  1. Watch the floor in real time. At every wedding there is a moment, I can almost time it, when the floor wobbles. Energy dips, three people drift to the bar. A DJ sees it two songs early and bends the set; a playlist plays track 47 regardless. In my experience, dance floors are not built by song choice but by sequence choice, made live.
  2. Handle the unplanned. Dinner overruns forty minutes. The best man's speech makes everyone cry and the room needs rebuilding. Someone requests the birthday girl's favourite song that nobody put on any list. Real events are improvised theatre; a playlist is a recording.
  3. Manage transitions and technology. Speeches need microphones faded over music, entrances need cues hit to the second, and silence, the phone that locks, the Bluetooth that drops, the advert that plays, needs to be impossible. The most common playlist disaster I hear about is not bad songs; it is dead air at the worst moment.
  4. Merge a mixed crowd. This is the big one at the weddings I play. Greek family, Belgian colleagues, teenagers, grandparents: no pre-built list satisfies that room, because satisfying it requires watching who dances to what and negotiating between tribes song by song. It is the difference between a jukebox and a host.

The middle path people forget

It does not have to be all-or-nothing. Two hybrids that work: hire a DJ for the party hours only and run your own playlist through his system during dinner. Many of my clients do exactly this, and I set the playlist up properly so even that part sounds right. Or, for corporate formats, book a shorter DJ set precisely where the agenda needs a human: the ignition hour after the speeches. Ask any professional about scoping down before ruling them out; the good ones would rather build you a smaller package than lose the evening to a phone. That flexibility is exactly why I offer everything from a solo party DJ setup to full production with sound, light, photo and video.

My verdict

Background music: playlist, with my blessing. A wedding, a milestone birthday, a company party where dancing is the point: a live DJ does a different job, and that job begins where the playlist's ends. The nights people still talk about years later were steered, not shuffled.
