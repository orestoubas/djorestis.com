The community circuit: Leuven to Lille

Notes from the road after back-to-back Greek community parties in Leuven and Lille — two cities, two crowds, one shared homeland on the dancefloor.

There is a version of this job that lives in glamorous venues, and there is the version I did this month: loading speakers into a van on a cold evening, driving the E40 to Leuven and, a few weeks later, crossing the border to Lille. The Greek community circuit will never make a film montage — but these nights are some of the most honest dancefloors I play all year.

Leuven: a university town finds its kefi

Leuven's Greek community is a lively blend — researchers and doctoral students, families who settled decades ago, and a rotating cast of students. Their party had that unmistakable community-hall warmth: home-made pites on trays, children weaving between the tables, and a committee of volunteers who had thought of everything except sitting down themselves.

Crowds like this ask for range. Within a single hour I went from gentle island songs for the grandparents' tables, to modern laika for the students, to one glorious stretch where a professor led a hasapiko with a precision that suggested decades of practice. The room was multigenerational in the truest sense: by the last hour the grandparents were teaching the students steps, not the other way round.

Lille: the border means nothing

A few weekends later, Lille. It surprises people that I regularly cross into France, but for the Greek diaspora the Belgian-French border is a formality — families and friendships stretch across it, and word of a good Greek night travels faster than any motorway. The Lille gathering had a slightly different flavour: more of a dinner-dance format, elegant tables, a proper meal — and then, once the plates were cleared, a transformation so fast I barely kept up with it. One moment coffee and conversation, the next a syrtos circle occupying every square metre of floor.

Different city, different hall, different accents — and the exact same moment when the first circle forms and every arm goes up.

Why I keep saying yes to community halls

  • These crowds listen with their memories. Play the right song and you are not a DJ any more; you are a time machine.
  • Community events are training for everything else. The reading skills I use at a corporate reception or a wedding were forged in rooms exactly like these.
  • The diaspora is a network, not a place. Leuven knows Lille, Lille knows Brussels, and half my bookings are born from someone's cousin seeing me play in another city.

The van rolls on

Antwerp, Ghent, Luxembourg, Maastricht — the circuit keeps growing, and my diary keeps filling with towns I had never played two years ago. If there are Greeks there, there is a party waiting to happen; it just needs someone to bring the sound.
