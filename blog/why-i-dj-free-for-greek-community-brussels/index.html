Playing for free, on purpose

Last week I DJed a community charity evening in Brussels without sending an invoice — here is the story, and the reasoning behind it.

Last week I packed the van, set up a full sound system, played for five hours and sent no invoice. It was a charity evening organised by women's and community associations within the Greek community of Brussels — the kind of gathering run entirely by volunteers, where the ticket money and the raffle go to people who need it. I have made it a quiet rule over the years: a few times a year, I play for the community for free. This post is about why.

The night itself

Community charity evenings have their own beautiful choreography. Long tables, home-cooked food arriving in relays from a kitchen full of volunteers, a raffle with prizes donated by every Greek business in town, speeches that run long because gratitude takes time. My role starts gently — warm background laika while people eat and catch up — and then, at exactly the right moment, the tables empty and the hall becomes what every Greek hall eventually becomes: a circle, hands joined, generations mixed.

That night the circle included ladies who have been organising community life in this city for longer than I have been alive, dancing next to teenagers who were born in Belgium and know the steps anyway. If you want to see how a diaspora survives eighty years, that circle is the answer.

Why free?

Three honest reasons:

  • Debt. The Greek community of Brussels gave me my first dancefloors, my first referrals, my first chances. Every paid booking I have — every wedding, every corporate gala — traces back, somewhere, to a community hall that took a chance on a young DJ. You do not invoice your roots.
  • Leverage. A volunteer-run charity evening has a budget measured in trays of spanakopita. The euros they would spend on a DJ do more good in the collection box. My equipment and my Saturday cost me little; to them it is the difference between a gathering and a celebration.
  • Perspective. Commercial work can slowly convince you that music is a product. One community night per season resets that. Nobody in that hall is a client. They are the reason the music exists.
Some sets build a career. These sets build the person who has the career.

An open offer

So let me put my rule in writing. If you run a recognised community, cultural or charitable association within the Greek community in Belgium and you are organising a fundraising or community event, ask me. If the calendar allows it, the answer is usually yes, and the invoice is usually zero. You can read more about my story and where I come from on my about page.

And if you are a commercial client reading this — this is also for you. The DJ who shows up for the community hall on Saturday is the same one who shows up for your gala on Friday, with the same heart. Get in touch either way; I answer everything personally.
