Case study

From reception mode to a full dance floor

A year-end evening for an international firm in Brussels where the formal programme had to land cleanly — and the room still had to end up dancing.

Client
International professional-services firm
Event
Year-end reception and staff party
Guests
Around 200
Location
Brussels, Belgium
Services
DJ, sound system, wireless microphones for speeches, dance-floor lighting

The brief

An international firm in Brussels sets aside one evening a year to thank its people. Around 200 guests, more than a dozen nationalities in the room, and a programme with real content in it: a welcome from leadership, speeches, and an awards moment. The brief from the event team was the one I hear most often — elegant first, but people have to actually dance.

Four things made that harder than it sounds:

  • Two events in one room. A standing reception with a formal programme, then a party — same space, same guests, roughly one hour apart.
  • Speech intelligibility. Awards and thank-yous mean nothing if the back of the room cannot hear them, and a system tuned only for music rarely handles a handheld microphone well.
  • A genuinely international audience. No single national catalogue of crowd-pleasers would have covered that room.
  • Colleagues, not strangers. Nobody bought a ticket, everyone is standing next to their manager, and no one wants to be the first person on an empty floor.

What I did

Planning started with the programme, not the playlist. I asked the event team for the running order in writing — doors, welcome, dinner or walking buffet, speeches, awards, and the intended end time — then agreed the practical detail directly with the venue and the caterer: power, access, noise limits, and when I could set up. Setup was finished and soundchecked well before doors, so guests never saw a cable being run.

  • Sound built for speech first. The system was configured so a wireless handheld sounds clear and controlled everywhere in the room; music then sits inside that same setup. One rig covers the whole evening, so there is no changeover.
  • Arrival and dinner at conversation level. Warm, low-profile programming that makes the room feel considered and lets people talk without raising their voices. Music ducked out cleanly for each speaker and came back underneath the applause.
  • A designed transition, not an accident. The move from programme to party was planned as a sequence: familiar, mid-tempo, recognisable songs first. The first dance-floor tracks are about permission, not discovery.
  • An international spine. A set that moves through international floor-fillers with deliberate nods across the room — a French classic, an Italian singalong, a Latin run, a Greek moment — so people hear that their side of the office was thought about. Announcements can run in Greek, English, French or Dutch as the room needs.
  • Reading the connectors. Every company has three or four people whose dancing gives everyone else permission. Those are the people the first twenty minutes of the party is played for.

The client took DJ plus sound and lighting so there was a single point of contact for the musical and technical side of the evening; the same evening can also run as a full package when a company would rather brief one supplier for everything.

What happened

On the night, the formal part ran without a microphone problem: speeches and the awards moment were audible from the back, and the music came back under the applause each time rather than cutting in cold.

The transition worked the way it is supposed to work when it has been built rather than hoped for. The first recognisable track pulled a handful of people out, the connectors followed within a song or two, jackets came off, and the floor filled after the speeches and stayed full through to the end of the evening.

  • Guests stayed to the close instead of drifting out once dinner was cleared.
  • The floor did not empty between tracks — the usual sign that the pacing is right.
  • Requests came in from several different nationalities in the room, which is the honest measure of whether an international set is working.
  • The event team had nothing to manage on the technical side once the evening started.
